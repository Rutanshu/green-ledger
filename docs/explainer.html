How your mockup becomes something people can actually use

1What you have right now

And why it isn't an app yet

✕ What you have

A drawing of an app

  • Looks exactly right
  • You can click between screens
  • Every number is typed in by hand
  • Nothing is saved — refresh and it's gone
  • There's no login
  • Only you can see it
✓ What you need

A working app

  • Looks the same
  • Anyone can open it from a link
  • Numbers are calculated from what people type
  • Everything is remembered, forever
  • People sign in and see only their own data
  • The whole world can use it at once

A mockup is a photograph of a car. An app is a car.

They look the same from the outside. Everything that makes one of them go is missing from the other.

The bit worth remembering

These are five separate companies, each doing one job, each free at your size. You don't buy “a server”. You rent five small services and wire them together. That's just how software is built now — it's why one person can put a real product online for nothing.

3What happens when someone clicks your link

Follow one person, one click, all the way through

Someone taps your link on LinkedIn

Their phone asks the internet: “where is greenledger.app?”

Vercel answersSOMEWHERE TO LIVE

Your code is sitting on Vercel's computers. It wakes up and starts building the page.

Your code asks the database a questionSOMEWHERE TO REMEMBER

“Which sites belong to this person, and how complete is each one?” Neon looks it up and answers.

The page is assembled and sent

Real numbers, from the database, dropped into the design you already made. Under a second.

They type 14,200 litres of diesel and hit save

It goes back to Vercel, which does the sum — 14,200 × 2.68 — and tells Neon to write it down.

It's there forever

They close the laptop. Come back in March. It's still 14,200 litres and still 38.06 tonnes.

That's the whole thing. Your mockup already does step 4 — it shows a page. It just shows made-up numbers instead of real ones. Steps 3 and 5 are the work.

4What “free” actually means

There is no catch, but there are edges

These companies give away a small amount for nothing, betting that if you grow, you'll pay them later. You get a real, public, working website — not a trial, not a watermark. Just with a ceiling.

Roughly what you get for £0

  • A few thousand people can try your tool per month
  • About half a gigabyte of saved data — thousands of records
  • 10 GB of uploaded files
  • Your own web address, if you buy one — about £10–30 a year, the only real money here

A LinkedIn post won't come close to those limits. If it somehow does, that's the good problem, and the fix is about $40/month.

Two edges to know about

EDGE 1  Vercel's free plan doesn't allow making money

A free demo anyone can try: fine. Charging for it, running ads, or even a donate button: not allowed on free — that's $20/month. Nothing breaks by accident; just know where the line is before you cross it.

EDGE 2  Some free databases fall asleep and don't wake up

Supabase — a popular choice — shuts down a free database after a week with no visitors. You post your link in September; someone clicks it in November; your site is broken and you don't know. Neon naps after 5 minutes but wakes up instantly on the next visitor. That single difference is the whole reason I picked Neon.

The shortcut, if the goal is just “does anyone want this?”

Build the Builder and the questionnaire and “show the maths”. Fake the dashboard with static numbers. That's about three weeks and it tells a complete story — someone can map a question to a factor, answer it, and watch a real emission number appear with the arithmetic exposed. Dashboards and exports impress nobody; that does.

6One more thing, and it matters more than you'd think

Nobody signs up for a stranger's tool. If your LinkedIn link lands on a login form, almost everyone leaves.

So build a “Try it” button that skips signup entirely. It hands the visitor a private practice copy, already filled with a realistic example company — a few sites, some finished, some half-done, a couple of deliberately broken things so there's something to fix. They poke around, break whatever they like, and it wipes itself clean overnight.

An empty product demos terribly. The single highest-value day of work in this whole project is the one where you write realistic fake data for that practice copy. More people will judge your tool on that than on any code you write.
